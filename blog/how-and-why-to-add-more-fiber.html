How — and Why — to Add More Fiber and Fermented Foods to Your Diet

Home / Blog / Fiber & Fermented Foods

By Ravi Kishore · Jan 03, 2026

Fiber: The Quiet Key To Better Health

People talk about protein all the time, carbs get unjustly blamed and fats are made more complex than they actually are in everyday fitness discourse. But there's one nutrient that quietly does a lot of important work and still gets ignored: fiber. It is a largely overlooked aspect of nutrition that even those claiming to be health conscious neglect a lot of the time.

Dietary Fiber or simply Fiber, is a type of carbohydrate derived from plant foods that your body cannot completely break down or absorb like fats, proteins and other carbohydrates like starches and sugars.

There are two types of fibers found in our everyday foods:

Soluble fibers (The 'soaks up water' fiber) — These lower the glucose levels in your body and aid in managing cholesterol. Examples: oats, beans, lentils

Insoluble fibers (The 'adds bulk' fiber) — These support the movement of foods in your digestive system, adding bulk to stool and preventing constipation. Examples: potatoes, whole-wheat flour, quinoa and almonds.

A survey released on World Digestive Health Day in 2025 revealed that approximately 70% of Indians don't meet their daily recommended fiber needs. A finding even more damning in the survey of women, with 74% falling short of the adequate intake compared to 64% of men. This is alarming because fiber plays a major role in long-term health.

Problems associated with low fiber intake

Fiber supports the healthy functioning of your body in a number of ways, it absorbs water which in turn adds bulk to stool and makes it easier to pass, lowering the chances of constipation. Without enough fiber in your daily eating habits, digestion can slow down, leading to constipation and irregular bowel movements. Over time, a low-fiber diet may also increase the risk of several health issues, including:

  • Diverticulosis
  • Chronic inflammation
  • Weight gain

Slow waste removal means harmful substances can stay in contact with the colon lining longer, which may increase cancer risk.

How to incorporate Fiber into your diet

The USDA's recommended daily amount of fiber intake for adults up to age 50 is 38 grams for men and 25 grams for women. Men and women older than 50 should be having 30 and 21 grams of fiber daily, respectively. Adding more fiber to your diet is mostly about choosing more whole, plant-based foods and making small adjustments to what you already eat every day. It is optimal to reach your daily intake through a variety of sources, ideally whole foods like vegetables, beans, fruits and nuts and seeds rather than relying on fiber supplements. While they can be helpful, they usually don't provide the full range of nutrients that naturally fiber-rich foods contain. Whole foods come with vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other beneficial compounds that support overall health. Another simple way to support your fiber intake, especially if you already enjoy dairy, is by adding fermented foods to your diet. Before we dive deeper into fermented foods, it will help to understand the gut microbiome because fiber and gut microbes are basically best friends.

Fermented Fiber and the Gut Microbiome

Gut Microbiome includes all the microorganisms like bacteria, fungi, viruses and other microbes that inhabit the gastrointestinal tract which is responsible for the breakdown of food and the absorption of nutrients. It also fights off various pathogens attacking the body. Everything we eat and even the place we live and the air we breathe affects what makes up the gut microbiome. Fermented fiber feeds beneficial gut bacteria, helping them produce short-chain fatty acids (like butyrate, acetate, and propionate) that support gut and metabolic health. This boosts microbial diversity, lowers colon pH, reduces inflammation, and helps offset the harms of low-fiber, processed diets. This fermentation of dietary fiber by microorganisms in the gut aids in the growth of beneficial bacteria and also has antiproliferative effects. A diet high in fermented foods enhances the gut microbiome which in turn boosts estrogen metabolism. Fermented foods or probiotics are simply foods containing bacteria which are added during the process of fermentation or in some cases, later on. (look for the words "live and active cultures" on the label). Yogurt is perhaps the most popular type of fermented food but there are a number of other choices like pickles, kimchi and kombucha. You can ease in gradually, beginning with a small serving of the formerly mentioned (yogurt, kefir, pickled vegetables) once daily and then increasing slowly. You can also consider your particular digestive sensitivities, choose options you tolerate well. Some people do better with dairy and others with vegetables. Since avoiding fiber long term can harm gut health, gradually adding both fiber and fermented foods helps restore microbial diversity while minimizing discomfort.
